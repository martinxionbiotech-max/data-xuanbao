# VOC Catalyst Deactivation: Poisoning, Fouling and Aging

**Direct answer:** VOC oxidation catalysts lose performance through chemical
poisoning (sulfur, halogens, silicones, heavy metals), surface fouling (polymerized
organics, particulates), and thermal aging (sintering, washcoat degradation). Most
failures are preventable by correct placement in the process and by keeping known
poisons away from the catalyst bed.

## The poisoning list

**Sulfur.** SO₂ and sulfur-containing VOCs (mercaptans, thiols, sulfides) poison
precious-metal sites. Sulfur adsorbs strongly and blocks CO and VOC oxidation.
Recovery is possible above ~300–350°C in some systems; below that, poisoning is
progressive.

**Halogens.** Chlorine and fluorine compounds (chlorinated solvents, halogenated
refrigerants) attack both the active metal and the washcoat. Halogen-bearing streams
require dedicated formulations and often a scrubbing stage upstream.

**Silicones.** Siloxanes (from adhesives, coatings, personal care products) are
devastating: combustion deposits silica on the surface, permanently masking sites.
A few ppm in the gas can kill a catalyst in weeks.

**Heavy metals.** Lead, zinc, mercury and others accumulate irreversibly, particularly
in incineration and metallurgy off-gas.

**Phosphorus.** Lubricant mists and flame retardants contribute phosphorus, another
accumulative poison.

## Fouling

**Polymerization.** Unsaturated VOCs (styrene, acrylates) can polymerize on the
catalyst surface at low temperature, forming a carbonaceous layer that blocks active
sites. The fix is keeping the operating temperature above the polymerization range
or periodically burning off the deposit.

**Particulates.** Dust and aerosol loading above design assumptions blinds the
catalyst face. Pre-filtration is cheaper than oversized catalyst.

## Thermal aging

- **Sintering** of the precious metal crystallites at sustained high temperature
  reduces active surface area — the dominant long-term mechanism above ~500°C.
- **Washcoat degradation** — phase changes and loss of surface area in the support.
- **Thermal shock** — rapid temperature swings crack monoliths and coatings,
  especially with frequent start-stop operation.

## Prevention checklist

1. Know the complete VOC species list — not just total concentration.
2. Identify the five poison families above in the stream before selecting a system.
3. Place the catalyst downstream of condensation, filtration and scrubbing where
   feasible.
4. Maintain temperature above polymerization range and below sintering range.
5. Monitor conversion at a fixed reference condition monthly — trending catches
   deactivation early.

## Manufacturer perspective

The single most valuable document we receive is the stream's species-level VOC
analysis. With it we can predict deactivation modes and design accordingly; without
it, every catalyst is a gamble. We ask for it before quoting non-standard
applications.
